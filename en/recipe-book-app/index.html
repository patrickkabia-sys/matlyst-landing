Recipe organizer

A cookbook app that keeps every recipe in one place

Screenshots, bookmarks, saved reels, your mom's recipe card. Matlyst puts them in one digital recipe book you can search, sort into folders, and cook from.

Matlyst is a recipe organizer app. Share a recipe from a website, an Instagram post, or a TikTok, or take a photo of one, and it becomes a clean list of ingredients and steps you can search, scale, plan, and shop from. It’s free to start.

Why I built it

I never trained as a chef. I trained as a refrigeration technician.

Patrick, founder of Matlyst. Food is my love language. I just wanted to stop losing the recipes I cook it from.

As an apprentice, my job took me into some of the best restaurant kitchens in my hometown. I was never the one cooking. I was the one fixing the walk-in cooler before the first order came in. From there I got a close look at how professionals work. Nothing is random. Everything has a place, a label, and a date.

At home, my recipes lived in eight places: notes, OneNote, a recipe app, bookmarks, TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, and favorites. I had the cooking, but no system. So I built the system I had seen in those kitchens, for a normal family dinner.

The rest of this page shows what that system does, step by step, and where it has limits.

What a recipe organizer app should actually do

Most people already have a recipe collection. It’s just scattered: a note here, a screenshot there, a saved video you can no longer find. A good recipe organizer does four jobs.

It gathers everything in one place

Recipes from websites, social posts, and photos are saved the same way, in folders you choose. Every recipe has the same layout, so you stop hunting through three apps to find the one you want.

It makes saved recipes findable

A collection you can’t search is just a bigger pile. Search should work on ingredients, because that’s how you decide what to cook on a Tuesday night.

It turns recipes into a plan

Add recipes to a meal plan, and the ingredients go to one grocery list, combined and sorted by store section. Read more about the meal plan and the grocery list from recipes.

In Matlyst

Put each recipe on the day you'll cook it.

Read more

It helps while you cook

Cook mode shows the recipe as one checklist you can tick off as you go. When a step mentions a time, Matlyst suggests a timer, it keeps running in the background, and the screen stays awake. Cook mode needs an internet connection.

Scale a recipe for more or fewer people

Change the number of servings from 1 to 20 and every amount scales with it. Scaling is linear, so check eggs, salt, and leavening yourself when you bake, because they don’t always scale in a straight line.

What Matlyst doesn’t do

A good tool says where it stops. Here is what to expect, so nothing surprises you in the kitchen.

  • It reads captions, not audio. Matlyst does not transcribe videos. If the amounts are only spoken, type them in after importing.
  • It doesn’t read comments. If a creator put the recipe in a comment or behind a link in the bio, copy it in by hand or share the link to the recipe page.
  • One recipe per post. If a reel shows three recipes, you will need to split them yourself.
  • Unit conversion only applies to new imports. With Metric, cups, ounces, and pounds are converted to milliliters and grams when you import. Recipes you have already saved keep their amounts.
  • AI can make mistakes. Matlyst uses AI to read and translate recipes, so check amounts and steps before you cook, especially from photos and handwriting.
  • Cook mode needs an internet connection. Reading a saved recipe works offline for up to 30 days after the last sync.

Common mistakes when organizing recipes

These are the ones that cost people the most time.

  • Saving without reading. Amounts are the part that goes wrong. Read the draft once, every time.
  • Keeping five copies. Pick one place. Add a note about what you changed so next time you don’t guess.
  • Saving only the video. You will rewatch it every time you cook. Save the recipe instead.
  • Building the perfect folder system first. Start with a few folders like Weeknights and Holidays, and add more only when you need them.
  • Forgetting the source. Keep the link to the creator on the recipe, so you can find their other recipes later.

Your recipes stay yours

You can export all your recipes any time, for free, as a Matlyst file (a zip with folders and photos) or as HTML. Moving from Paprika? Import your Paprika export, and your categories become folders.

You can read your recipes and grocery list offline for up to 30 days after the last sync. Editing and cook mode need a connection.

How to move 100 recipes in one weekend

A big collection feels like a big job, so people put it off for years. It isn’t one big job. It’s a hundred small ones, and most of them take under two minutes. The trick is to do them in a fixed order, so you never stop to decide what comes next.

Friday evening: gather and sort (45 minutes)

Don’t import anything yet. Open every place your recipes live: the Saved tab on Instagram, TikTok Favorites, your bookmarks, your notes app, your camera roll, the drawer with the printed cards. In each place, scroll once and decide, recipe by recipe, whether you have cooked it or truly want to. Be strict. A recipe you saved three years ago and never opened is a memory, not a plan.

Write the keepers down in three piles: cooked and loved, cooked once, would repeat, and want to try. You can do this on paper. Aim for a list you can finish, not a list that makes you feel good.

Saturday morning: import the loved pile (about 90 minutes)

Start with the recipes you already cook. You know what they should taste like, so you will catch a wrong amount at a glance. Share each one to Matlyst, read the recipe it builds, fix what is off, and put it in a folder. On the free plan you get 5 imports per calendar month, so a move this size needs Matlyst Pro. Websites usually come through cleanest. Posts from Instagram and TikTok depend on what the creator wrote in the caption. Do the website recipes first and the social posts second, because the second group needs more reading.

Keep the pace steady and work in batches of ten. After each batch, stand up, get water, and look at what you saved. A short break resets your eyes, and amounts are exactly what tired eyes miss.

Saturday afternoon: handle the awkward ones (60 minutes)

These are the recipes that do not import in one tap: the handwritten cards, the posts where the amounts are only spoken, the recipe someone texted you. For cards and printed pages, use Take photo on the Add tab, a Matlyst Pro feature that reads up to 20 photos at a time, and compare every amount with the original. For a spoken recipe, import the post, then type the amounts you heard. For a text message, paste it under Type it in.

Sunday: plan one week, then stop

This is the step that proves the system works. Pick five recipes from your new collection, add them to the meal plan, and open the grocery list. If the list looks right, you are done. If something is off, you found it with five recipes instead of a hundred.

Do not import the “want to try” pile in the same weekend. Leave it in your old places, and move a recipe over only when you add it to a plan. That way your collection stays a list of food you actually make, and the “want to try” pile can’t bury it.

A folder structure that survives

Most folder systems die for one reason: they are built for a collection you wish you had. Build folders for the collection you have. A good rule is that a folder should hold enough recipes to be worth opening, and few enough that you can scan it without searching.

Pick one way to divide, not three

You can sort by meal (Weeknights, Weekends, Breakfast), by main ingredient (Chicken, Fish, Vegetarian), by occasion (Holidays, Birthdays), or by source (Grandma, Instagram). The mistake is mixing these in one level. If “Chicken” and “Weeknights” are both top-level folders, a chicken weeknight dinner belongs in both, and you will put it in one and look in the other.

Choose the question you ask most often when you are hungry. For most people it is “what kind of dinner is this?” That makes meal type the right top level. You can find the main ingredient with search, which is faster than any folder.

A starter set

  • Weeknights: 30 minutes or less, one pan, few ingredients.
  • Weekends: longer cooks, projects, things for guests.
  • Baking: anything where precision matters.
  • Holidays: the recipes that come out once a year, which are the ones you most often lose.
  • Family: cards and recipes from people, with the person’s name in the title or a note.

Five folders is enough to start. Add a sixth only when you catch yourself saving three recipes in a row that have no home.

When a folder gets too big

If a folder holds more than you can scan, split it by the thing that actually separates the recipes in it. A Weeknights folder that has grown large might become “Weeknights: pasta” and “Weeknights: sheet pan.” Do the split during a quiet moment, not while you are hungry, and move recipes in one sitting so you do not end up with half the recipes in each place.

Use favorites for the short list

Folders answer “what kind of food.” Favorites answer “what do I trust.” Mark the recipes you would cook for guests without a test run. After a few months, that short list is the one you open most.

Names and notes: how to mark a recipe you changed

Every recipe you cook more than twice gets changed. You use less sugar, swap the herb, bake it five minutes longer in your oven. If you do not write that down, you will rediscover it next time, usually after a worse dinner.

Keep the source on the recipe

Matlyst keeps the source link on every imported recipe, and the title is written in the language you use the app in. The link makes it easy to find the original and give credit. Put your own changes in My notes on the recipe, and keep the title as a name you will search for.

Write notes in a fixed pattern

A note you can scan at 6 p.m. beats a paragraph you have to read. A pattern that works:

  • Changed: what you did differently, such as “half the sugar, extra lemon.”
  • Why: one short reason, such as “too sweet for the kids.”
  • Next time: one thing to try, such as “add chili at the end.”

Date the note if the change took several tries. “Third try” tells you the recipe is settled.

Edit the recipe, or save a copy

If you want to keep the original untouched, ask Ask Matlyst, a Pro feature, for the change and choose Save a copy. The copy links back to the source and is marked “adapted,” so you always see which version is the creator’s and which is yours. If you are simply correcting your own recipe, choose Replace. For a change you make by hand, edit the ingredient or step directly and put the reason in a note.

Ask Matlyst uses AI, and it can get things wrong, so read the changed amounts before you cook from a copy.

Mark the recipes you have fixed after a bad import

If you corrected an amount because the draft was wrong, add one line to the note: “Amounts checked against the original.” When you open the recipe months later, you know you can trust it. A recipe without that line is one you have not yet cooked from.

Different recipes go wrong in different places. Knowing where to look makes your check of the draft faster and more reliable.

Before you cook, and after you cook

Most of what makes a kitchen feel calm happens before the heat goes on. Professional kitchens work this way: everything is ready, labeled, and in reach before service starts. You can do a small version of it at home, in about the time it takes the pan to heat.

Before you cook

  1. Read the whole recipe once. A step like “marinate for two hours” is only useful if you read it at 2 p.m., not at 6.
  2. Set the servings. Change the number first, so every amount you read is the right one.
  3. Check the pantry and the fridge. Compare the ingredients with what you have. If you keep a pantry in Matlyst, the recipe marks the ingredients you already have. Anything missing goes on the grocery list, or gets a swap.
  4. Take out and measure. Put the ingredients on the counter in the order you will use them. This is the idea behind mise en place: what you need is ready before you start.
  5. Tap Start cooking. Cook mode shows the recipe as a checklist, keeps the screen awake, and suggests timers from the step text. It loads the recipe over the internet, so open it while you still have signal.

While you cook

Tick off steps as you finish them, so a phone call does not cost you your place. Treat a suggested timer as a starting point. Your oven and your pan are not the creator’s, so judge by color, smell, and texture too.

After you cook

  1. Write the note while you remember. One line: what you changed and what you would do next time. Thirty seconds now saves a rediscovery later.
  2. Mark a favorite if it earned one. Be honest. If you would not serve it to guests, it is not a favorite yet. If you keep a pantry, tap Update pantry in cook mode to remove what you used.
  3. Put leftovers away promptly. The fridge should be at 40°F (4°C) or colder, and the freezer at 0°F (-18°C). According to FoodSafety.gov, cooked meat and poultry leftovers keep for 3 to 4 days in the fridge.
  4. Label what you freeze. Name and date, always. The label you write today is the one that makes the oldest portion go first.

When the draft looks wrong, check these

A bad draft is normal, and it is not a reason to give up on an app. Most problems come from the source, not the import. Work through these in order before you start retyping.

The amounts are missing

Open the original post or page. If the amounts are not there, they were never written down, and no app can invent them. If they are only spoken in a video, remember that Matlyst reads captions, not audio. Type what you hear, and add a note that you did.

Half the recipe is missing

Open the post and read the caption to the end. If the full recipe is there and the import still missed part of it, copy the caption and paste it under Type it in. If the recipe is in a comment, Matlyst does not read comments, so copy that text and paste it the same way. If it is behind a link in the bio, share the recipe page that link opens.

Two recipes got mixed together

Matlyst imports one recipe per post. If a reel shows several dishes, split them yourself: delete the second dish from the imported recipe, then add it as its own recipe under Type it in.

The units look unfamiliar

Look at Units in Settings. It has two choices, Metric and US, and it applies to new imports. With Metric, cups, ounces, and pounds from the source are converted to metric. With US, Matlyst keeps the source’s measurements and never converts between US and metric, so a recipe written in grams stays in grams. If the units look wrong, check the setting, then import the recipe again.

The steps are out of order, or one is a joke

Edit the steps directly. Delete anything that is not an action. Cook mode shows one checklist, so every extra line costs you a moment when your hands are full.

The photo import misread a number

This is the most common problem with handwriting and old print. Check every fraction and every temperature against the original, and retake the photo in better light if several lines are wrong. A photo taken straight from above, with no shadow across the page, reads best.

You are still not sure

If you cannot trust the draft after one pass, treat the recipe as unverified. Cook it once with the original beside you, correct what you learn, and write “checked” in My notes afterward.

Kitchen habits that make a collection easier to keep

An organized collection is not a one-time project. It is a few small habits that cost almost nothing. These are borrowed from kitchens where a mistake is expensive, and scaled down to a family dinner.

Everything has a place

In a restaurant, you never ask where the butter is. A recipe collection works the same way. If you cannot say in one sentence which folder a recipe belongs in, the folder system needs a fix, not the recipe.

Raw meat goes on the bottom shelf

This is a fridge rule that also works as a planning rule. Plan the dinners with raw meat and fish early in the week, and cook them first. Store raw meat on the lowest shelf so nothing drips onto food that will not be cooked, and keep the fridge at 40°F (4°C) or colder.

Rotate your stock

Put the new thing behind the old thing. The same idea applies to recipes: when you add one, look at the oldest recipe in the folder and ask whether you have cooked it this year. If not, either schedule it or let it go.

Label and date

A container of leftovers without a date is a guessing game. Write the name and the day, and eat cooked meat and poultry within 3 to 4 days. Anything in the freezer should have a label too. Recipe notes with a date do the same job for your collection.

Do a small review every month

Once a month, spend ten minutes in your collection. Delete a recipe you will never cook, fix one with a wrong amount, and move one “want to try” into a plan. Ten minutes a month keeps a collection from turning back into a pile.
