How to save recipes from Instagram

Found a recipe on Instagram? Share the public post or reel with Matlyst. The app uses the available caption to create an editable recipe with separate ingredients and instructions.

Open the post or reel, tap Share, choose Matlyst, and check the draft recipe it builds from the caption. Save it, and it becomes a searchable recipe with ingredients and steps, not a bookmark you will never find again. This works when the recipe is written in the caption.

Three ways people save Instagram recipes, and where each breaks

Most people use one of these. All three work for a while, and then you need the recipe.

Instagram’s own Save button

It’s quick, and the post stays in your Saved tab. The catch is that it’s a bookmark, not a recipe. You can’t search by ingredient, scale servings, or put it on a shopping list, and if the creator deletes the post, it’s gone.

A screenshot or a copied caption

You keep the text, but a screenshot of a long caption is hard to read later, and copied text is a wall of words in a notes app. Neither can be searched the way a real recipe can.

Saving the reel to your camera roll

You keep the video, but the amounts are often only spoken or flashed on screen for a second. You rewatch it every time you cook.

Sharing straight to a recipe app

The post becomes a structured recipe with ingredients, amounts, and numbered steps. That is what the rest of this guide shows.

How to save a recipe from Instagram, step by step

  1. Open the post or reel that has the recipe in its caption.
  2. Tap the Share icon (the paper airplane), then choose Share to and pick Matlyst from the app list. If you don’t see it right away, scroll the app list.
  3. Wait a moment while Matlyst reads the caption.
  4. Read the draft. Compare the amounts and steps with the post.
  5. Fix anything that looks off, pick a folder, and save.

Prefer not to use the share menu? Copy the post link, open Add recipe in Matlyst, and choose Paste a link. If you can copy the recipe text but the link can’t be read, paste the text under Type it in.

What we found when we tested it

On November 18, 2026, we imported two real reels. A short caption with 5 ingredients and 5 steps came through as 5 of 5 ingredients and 5 of 5 steps. A long caption of 1,716 characters, with 18 ingredients in three groups, came through as 18 of 18 ingredients and 5 of 5 steps, even though Instagram cuts long captions off with “… more.”

That is two reels, not a promise. Captions vary, so read the draft every time. Amounts are the part that goes wrong.

When the recipe is not in the caption

Some creators put the recipe in a comment, behind a link in the bio, or only in the video. Here is what Matlyst can and can’t do.

  • Recipe in a comment or a link in the bio. Matlyst doesn’t read comments. Copy the recipe from the comment and paste it under Type it in, or share the link if it goes to a recipe page.
  • Recipe only spoken in the video. It does not transcribe speech or text shown only inside a reel. Watch once, then add the amounts yourself after importing.
  • Several recipes in one reel. Matlyst imports one recipe per post. Split them by hand.
  • Private, deleted, or restricted posts. Matlyst needs access to a public post, so these may not be readable.

What to check in the draft before you save

Reading the draft takes a minute, and it is the step that decides whether the recipe works. Check these in order.

  1. Amounts and units. Does each ingredient have a number? A missing amount usually means the creator didn’t write one. Matlyst uses metric by default, so switch Units to US in Settings if you want cups and ounces kept as the source wrote them. Matlyst does not convert between them.
  2. Ingredient groups. Long captions often split ingredients into groups, such as sauce, filling, and topping. Make sure nothing landed in the wrong group.
  3. Oven temperature and times. These are easy to miss in a long caption. Compare them with the post.
  4. The steps. Are they in order, and is every step something you do? Cut anything that is a joke or a hashtag.
  5. Language. If the caption was in another language, the recipe is translated to English. Look at ingredient names you don’t recognize.

Matlyst uses AI to read and translate recipes, and AI can make mistakes. That is why the draft comes first and saving comes second.

After you save: what you can do with the recipe

A saved recipe is a normal Matlyst recipe, which means you get everything else too.

  • Scale it from 1 to 20 servings.
  • Plan it. Add it to the meal plan, and send the ingredients to one grocery list, combined and sorted by store section.
  • Cook from it. Cook mode shows the steps as a checklist and suggests timers from the step text. It needs an internet connection.
  • Adapt it. With Ask Matlyst, a Pro feature, you can ask for a vegan, quicker, or smaller version and save it as a copy.
  • Take it with you. Export every recipe for free as a Matlyst file or HTML.

Matlyst is free to start, with 5 imports per calendar month. Matlyst Pro adds more imports and photo import.

The real problem: no amounts

The most common reason a saved Instagram recipe is useless is that the creator never wrote down the amounts. Matlyst can’t invent them, and it shouldn’t. When you see “salt and pepper” with no quantity, or only “a splash of soy,” treat the draft as a starting point. Taste, adjust, and save your own amounts so next time you don’t guess.

How an Instagram recipe caption is usually built

Once you have read a few hundred captions, you notice that most follow the same loose shape. Knowing the shape makes it easy to spot, in a second, what is missing from the draft Matlyst builds.

  1. A hook line. “The easiest weeknight pasta.” It is not part of the recipe, and it can safely end up in the description or be deleted.
  2. An ingredient list. Sometimes a tidy list, sometimes a run of lines with emoji bullets, sometimes one paragraph with commas. Long lists are often split into groups: sauce, filling, topping.
  3. The method. Numbered steps, or short paragraphs that read like steps. This is where oven temperature and time usually hide.
  4. Notes. “Serves 4,” “keeps 3 days in the fridge,” “swap the cream for coconut milk.” Useful, and easy to overlook.
  5. Hashtags and tags. Not part of the recipe at all.

What to look for in the draft

Open the draft next to the post and check five things, in this order:

  • Ingredient groups. If the caption had “For the sauce” and “For the topping,” the same split should appear in the draft.
  • Amounts. Every ingredient that needs a quantity should have one. “Salt” and “oil” often have none, because the creator wrote “to taste.”
  • Oven temperature. Look for it in the steps. If the caption said 400°F, the step should say 400°F, not a rounded or missing number.
  • Time. Minutes for baking, simmering, and resting. A missing time is a common gap.
  • “To taste.” Seasoning with no amount is normal and not an error. The recipe is more useful if you add your own amount after you cook it once.

Before you trust it: a ten-second checklist

The draft is a first pass. When you paste a link on the Add tab, you see the recipe before you tap Save recipe. When you share from Instagram, the recipe can be saved in the background, so you check it right after. Either way, run through this list once. After a few recipes it becomes automatic.

  • Does the title say what the dish is, in words you would search for later?
  • Is every ingredient listed with an amount, or on purpose without one?
  • Do the number of servings and the amounts belong together? Scaling uses the servings number, so a wrong one throws off every quantity.
  • Are the steps in the order you would cook them, and is each one an action?
  • Are temperature and time written out in the step they belong to?
  • Is the recipe in a folder you will look in?
  • Is the source still attached, so you can go back to the post?

If you can say yes to all seven, you are done. If one is a no, fix it now. It takes less time than it will take on a busy Tuesday when the pan is already hot.

The import came out wrong: what to do, step by step

Imports from captions are not perfect, and it is better to know the usual causes than to guess. Find your symptom below.

The recipe came out half empty

  1. Open the post in Instagram and scroll the caption to the end. Is the full recipe actually written there?
  2. If part of it is in the first comment, Matlyst does not read comments. Copy the caption and the comment together, and paste them under Type it in on the Add tab, or add the missing lines to the imported recipe by hand.
  3. If the recipe is behind a link in the creator’s bio, open the link. When it goes to a recipe page, share that page to Matlyst instead.
  4. If the recipe is only in the video, add the amounts yourself after importing.

Amounts are missing

First check whether the creator wrote them. If the caption has “1 cup rice” and the draft has “rice,” edit the line and add the cup. If the caption has no amount, there is nothing to import. Add your own after you cook it, and the recipe is better than the original.

The steps are out of order, or there are too many

Long captions sometimes mix a tip into the method. Read the steps from top to bottom as if you were cooking. Move a step to where it belongs, merge two small ones, or delete anything that is not an action.

The language is wrong

When a caption is in another language, Matlyst translates the recipe into the language you use the app in. Most of it reads naturally, but ingredient names can come out odd, especially regional ones. Compare any name you do not recognize with the post, and rename it to what your store calls it.

It says the post cannot be read

Matlyst needs a public post. If the post was deleted or made private, Matlyst cannot find it. If the creator has limited the post to viewing inside Instagram, the text cannot be read either. In both cases, copy the recipe text from the post and paste it under Type it in.

There is more than one recipe in the post

Matlyst makes one recipe per post. For a post with a main dish and a dessert, import once, then split the dessert into its own recipe by hand.

Building a collection from Instagram without drowning

The goal is not to save more. It is to be able to find the right recipe at five o’clock on a weeknight. A small habit does most of the work.

A weekly routine that takes ten minutes

  1. During the week, use Instagram’s own Save button freely. It costs nothing and keeps the pile in one place.
  2. Once a week, open your Saved tab and pick the two or three recipes you actually want to cook.
  3. Share only those to Matlyst, read each draft, and save.
  4. Put each one in a folder right away. Folders you will look in, such as Weeknight, Baking, or Guests, beat a perfect system you never open.

This also fits the free plan, which includes 5 imports per calendar month. If you cook from Instagram a lot, Matlyst Pro adds more.

Name recipes for how you will search

A creator’s title is often a hook, like “The pasta that broke the internet.” Rename it to what you would type into search: “Baked feta pasta.” You can search by ingredient too, so the ingredient list does part of the work for you.

Keep the collection small on purpose

If a recipe has sat untouched for months, ask whether you will ever cook it. Delete it without guilt. The creator’s post is still on Instagram if you change your mind.

What if the creator deletes the post?

It happens. Accounts get closed, and creators remove posts. If you only bookmarked it, it is gone. If you imported it into Matlyst, the recipe you saved is still there: the ingredients, the steps, and any changes you made. What may stop working is the link back to the original post.

That is one of the main reasons to save the recipe itself and not just the post. It is also why the source badge matters. While the post exists, it lets you go back to the creator. When it does not, the recipe still stands on its own.

Respect the creator, and keep the source

A recipe in a caption is someone’s work. A few habits keep it fair.

  • Keep the source on the recipe. Matlyst attaches the link to the post and shows the creator’s handle when it can find it, or the platform name when it cannot. Do not remove it.
  • Use it for cooking, not for reposting. A saved recipe is for your kitchen. If you want to share the dish, link to the original post.
  • Credit your changes. If you change the recipe a lot, say so in My notes on the recipe. The next section shows how Matlyst marks an adapted copy.

Common misunderstandings

  • “It watches the video.” It does not. It reads the caption text. Speech, on-screen text, and music are not read.
  • “It reads the comments.” It does not. A recipe pinned in a comment has to be copied over by you.
  • “It downloads the reel.” It saves the recipe and a link to the source, not the video file.
  • “Units are always kept as written.” That depends on Units in Settings. With Metric, cups, ounces, and pounds are converted to metric. With US, Matlyst keeps the source’s measurements. The setting applies to new imports.
  • “AI means it is always right.” AI reads and translates the caption, and it can make mistakes. The draft step exists for that reason.
  • “One post, many recipes.” One post becomes one recipe.

If you are comparing ways to keep recipes at all, the guide to choosing a recipe organizer app walks through what matters. To bring in recipes from websites too, see import recipes.

Common mistakes

  • Saving without checking. Always read the draft once. Amounts are what goes wrong.
  • Trusting a screenshot. It is a picture, not a recipe.
  • Saving only the reel. You will rewatch it every time.
  • Not keeping the source. Leave the creator’s link on the recipe.

Instagram recipe questions

How do I import recipes from Instagram?

Open a public Instagram post or reel, tap Share, choose Share to and select Matlyst. Review the imported ingredients and instructions before saving.

Is there a free app to import recipes from Instagram?

Matlyst includes a limited number of free link imports each month. Current limits, Pro features and prices are shown in the app before you buy.

How do I extract a recipe from an Instagram reel?

Share the public reel with Matlyst. If the recipe is available in its caption, Matlyst can organise it into ingredients and instructions. It does not transcribe speech or text shown only inside the reel.

How can I convert an Instagram reel to a recipe?

Share the reel or paste its link in Matlyst. The app turns accessible recipe text into an editable recipe you can organise, plan and shop from.

Where are imported Instagram recipes saved?

They are saved in your Matlyst recipe collection, not in Instagram. You can find them with search, organise them in folders and keep the original source link when it is available.

Can I copy a recipe from an Instagram caption on iPhone?

Yes. You can share the post directly to Matlyst, or copy accessible recipe text and paste it under Type it in.

Can Matlyst import recipes from private Instagram accounts?

Not reliably. Matlyst needs access to the recipe information in a public post. Private, deleted, restricted or unavailable posts may not be readable.

Does Matlyst download the Instagram video?

No. Matlyst saves the structured recipe information and keeps the source link when available. It does not download the reel as a video file.
